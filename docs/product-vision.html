Where do my cells sit?

A habit worth having, and the loop that pays for it.

← all documents · Kat Titterton · San Francisco · 25 September 2026

Vision

Every lab running a cell line should know, every time it images a plate, where its cells sit among everyone else's, and what to do if they have moved. The model is Strava's segments: your run placed among everyone who ran the same stretch of road. Here the road is the cell line, and the field is every lab that ran it.

The product earns its habit the way a bathroom scale or a credit score does: the number is about you, it changes, and nobody knows it until they look. It never earns it the way a feed does. Everything below serves one rule: uploading must be effortless, and the answer must be worth looking at.

02Six magic moments

Each moment turns an image the lab already has into something it could not see before, and each is ordered by when a new customer meets it.

# Moment What the lab sees When
1 Your past, diagnosed old plates dragged in on day one appear as a path across the field map: "you left the envelope in March" first ten minutes
2 Show the cells, not the score when a plate moves, its nuclei next to their nearest neighbours from labs in distribution: same size, more texture every flag
3 The fix what the in-distribution labs do differently: seeding density, media or serum lot after a flag
4 A forecast "at your current drift, this line leaves the envelope in about five weeks" after three readings
5 Someone else's plate saves your screen a lot you use is drifting at other labs, before your own plates show it once labs share lots
6 The certificate as currency a verifiable link for a methods section, a CRO handoff, a grant report when results leave the lab

Moments 1 to 4 work for a single lab on day one, against the public reference. Moments 5 and 6 exist only once the network does, and no competitor can copy them without the labs.

03The revenue loop

The habit loop feeds the revenue loop. Free contributors widen the reference; a wider reference sharpens every certificate and makes bad-lot alerts possible; sharper answers are what pharma and CROs pay for; paid and shared certificates spread through vendors and reviewers, which brings more labs in.

Buyer Pays for Proposed price Why they keep paying
Academic labs and cores nothing, if they contribute free for contributors, \~$500–1,000 per line-quarter otherwise their own trajectory and the diagnosis of their past
Pharma and CRO screening certificates, forecasts, the fix \~$5–15k per line-quarter, or a site license one late-caught line cost \~$2.6M; a quarterly reading is insurance
Cell banks and vendors a lot certificate to ship with each lot per lot customers start asking for it
Everyone, later bad-lot alerts subscription only the network can produce them

The quarter is a starting cadence: in the one lab measured, a baseline is stale within weeks. Pricing is proposed, not validated; the roadmap doc carries the anchors.

04A healthy habit, not a trap

The product dies the day a lab hides its worst plate, because the worst plates teach the field most. Every design choice protects honest uploads.

Do Don't Why
Show each lab its position privately; publish only the field public leaderboards of labs shame makes labs withhold bad plates, which poisons the reference
Give credit for uploading flagged plates penalise being out of distribution failures are the most valuable data
Keep a record of certified quarters "don't break your streak" nudges a streak rewards hiding the plate that would break it
Explain every flag with cells and a suggested fix a bare red dot a verdict without a reason feels like judgement, not help
Let the instrument trigger uploads notifications to come back and check the reward is the answer, not the visit

05The end state: from loved to required

The strongest habit is one an institution asks for. Identity testing got there when journals and funders began asking labs to show their cells were what they claimed. A behaviour certificate can follow the same path: first reviewers ask for it, then CRO contracts include it, then a vendor lot ships with it by default.

That lock-in has a cost: a required certificate reads as compliance, and compliance is rarely loved. So the order matters. Build the loved product first (the map, the cells, the fix, the warning) so that when it becomes required, labs already want it.
